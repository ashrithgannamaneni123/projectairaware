The problem

Why asthma hits harder here.

Three things stack up in Mountain House.

  • A higher rateOur county's childhood asthma rate runs well above California's.
  • Worse airOur air basin ranks among the most polluted in the country.
  • A young city42% of residents are 18 or younger — the group most affected.

Why the air is like this

  • 8 of 8 Valley countiesrank in California's top 10 for overall pollution burden.
  • Top 10 statewide for PM2.5San Joaquin County — and in the top half for ozone.
  • ~5% of vehicles, ~70% of NOxHeavy trucks and farm equipment produce about seven times the smog-forming pollution of the Valley's ~3 million cars combined.

What we're doing about it

Phase 1

Awareness

Seven anonymous questions, asked at community events and outside local businesses — then the identical survey again after the campaign, so the result is measured rather than claimed.

Phase 2

Prevention

Trigger education with Mountain House schools, aimed at the 5–17 age group the county data says gets hit hardest. We've also presented to the City Council to make the partnership last.

Where these numbers come from

Why county data instead of Mountain House data?

Because Mountain House data doesn't exist yet. These are the most recent county-level figures the California Department of Public Health has published. Our city only incorporated in 2024, and nobody has measured asthma here on its own — which is a large part of why our survey matters.

Sources for the air quality figures

Pollution-burden rankings, PM2.5 and ozone standings, and the heavy-duty vehicle emissions comparison describe the San Joaquin Valley air basin and San Joaquin County. The characterization of the region's air quality as among the nation's worst comes from the EPA.

Recognizing an asthma attack

Four signs to know — and the two things to do if you see them.

Wheezing
Coughing
Shortness of breath
Chest tightness
Their own inhaler A person with asthma should use their own prescribed inhaler, exactly as their doctor directed. Never share an inhaler.
Call 911 If the symptoms are severe, or they don't improve, call 911 straight away.

Sources: CDC; Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America. This is a student awareness project — not medical advice, and not a substitute for a doctor.
